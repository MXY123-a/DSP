# DSP-Lab Reflection Scoring Rubric

## Purpose

Each DSP-Lab activity ends with a short written reflection. This rubric converts the reflection text into a structured qualitative/quantitative indicator that can be used as a secondary research variable.

The rubric is designed to reward explanation and evidence rather than writing length. A long response should not automatically receive a high score.

## Scoring dimensions

Score each reflection on four dimensions from 0 to 3.

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Conceptual accuracy** | No relevant DSP concept or fundamentally incorrect | Mentions a relevant concept but contains major misunderstanding | Mostly correct with minor imprecision | Correct and appropriately uses the key DSP concept |
| **Use of experimental evidence** | No reference to observed result or parameter change | Vague reference to what was seen | Refers to a specific plot, value, case, or parameter comparison | Uses specific observations/values to support the conclusion clearly |
| **Causal explanation** | Only states what happened | Gives a weak or unsupported reason | Provides a mostly correct DSP explanation | Clearly explains why the observed behavior occurs using the relevant DSP mechanism |
| **Comparison / transfer** | No comparison or implication | Simple comparison without interpretation | Meaningful comparison between two cases/parameter settings | Connects multiple cases and draws a generalizable conclusion or prediction |

### Total score

`Reflection score = Conceptual accuracy + Evidence + Causal explanation + Comparison/transfer`

Range per lab: **0–12**

For five completed labs, an optional total reflection score ranges from **0–60**.

## Lab-specific anchors

### Lab 01 — Discrete-time signals

A strong response should correctly explain how changing amplitude, frequency, phase, or signal type changes the sequence, and should refer to observed waveform or quantitative measures such as peak-to-peak value or mean.

### Lab 02 — Sampling & aliasing

A strong response should connect the Safe/Boundary/Aliasing cases to the Nyquist criterion and explain how undersampling can make a higher analog frequency appear as a lower discrete-time frequency.

### Lab 03 — Discrete convolution

A strong response should describe convolution in terms of shifted overlap, products, and summation, and should use at least one selected output sample or the smoothing example as evidence.

### Lab 04 — DFT & spectrum

A strong response should explain the relationship between `Fs`, `N`, and frequency-bin spacing, and distinguish improved resolution from spectral leakage caused by a non-bin-centered tone.

### Lab 05 — FIR low-pass filter

A strong response should explain how cutoff frequency and tap count influence passband/stopband behavior or transition sharpness, using the observed impulse/frequency response as evidence.

## Coding procedure for the study

Recommended procedure:

1. Remove names and use only anonymous participant codes.
2. Two raters independently score a subset of approximately 20–30% of reflections.
3. Resolve major disagreements after the initial independent coding.
4. Report inter-rater reliability for the double-coded subset. Weighted Cohen's kappa can be used for the 0–3 ordinal dimension scores; an ICC may be used for the total 0–12 score if justified.
5. After acceptable agreement is established, one trained rater may score the remaining reflections using the agreed rubric.

## Suggested use in analysis

Reflection score should be treated as a **secondary engagement/conceptual-explanation indicator**, not as the primary learning outcome.

Possible analyses within the DSP-Lab group include:

- correlation between total reflection score and post-test score;
- correlation between reflection score and normalized/raw learning gain;
- comparison of reflection score across Labs 01–05;
- qualitative examples illustrating common correct explanations or misconceptions.

Do not select quotations only because they support the hypothesis. If qualitative excerpts are reported, use a transparent coding approach and include representative positive, partial, and misconception patterns where appropriate.
